The problem

Changing your major
shouldn't mean starting over.

Students change their majors, minors and academic plans all the time — but understanding what actually happens to their degree progress can be surprisingly difficult.

Existing degree audit tools are designed to show you where you are, not what happens if you change direction.
See the solution ↓

Some courses still count. Some quietly stop counting. Most students find out late.

What goes wrong

The hidden cost of changing direction

Three problems compound into one: by the time the consequences are visible, they have already happened.

01

Unclear degree audits

Traditional audits tell students whether courses currently count toward their degree, but rarely make it easy to explore a hypothetical scenario.

02

Uncertainty around lost credits

When switching majors, students may not know which completed courses still count, which become electives, and which no longer contribute to graduation.

03

The domino effect

Changing one course or major affects prerequisites, sequencing and graduation requirements — and ultimately time and tuition.

  1. Major change

  2. Different requirements

  3. Courses no longer apply

  4. Additional courses

  5. Delayed graduation, higher cost

National Center for Education Statistics · 2017

Major change is normal, not rare

Roughly a third of students in bachelor's programmes changed major within three years, and 35% of those who started in a STEM field changed course, compared with 29% outside STEM.

Community College Research Center, Columbia · 2020

The cost shows up in credits

Liu, Mishra and Kopko found switching can delay completion through excess credits — or improve it through a better academic match. Their recommendation: don't discourage switching, plan for it, with flexible application of credits so students can revise their goals without losing time and tuition.

Read the research ↗

Discover Education · Open access · June 2026

It is a transition, not a form

Higgerson interviewed 13 undergraduates who changed major. Students described stress and uncertainty alongside optimism, and leaned on advising, institutional resources and online catalogue tools to work out where they stood.

Time

Money

Fewer unnecessary credits can mean lower tuition cost. Research links major switching to excess credit accumulation — the potential saving depends on the institution.

Confidence

Students can explore an academic change with a clearer understanding of what follows from it, before committing.
